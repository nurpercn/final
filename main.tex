{\Large \bf Integrated Sample Size Optimization and Test Chamber Scheduling under Environmental Constraints}\\
\vskip 12pt
\normalsize
Nur Per\c{c}in$^{1}$,
Ali Ekici$^{1}$\\[6pt]
\footnotesize
$^{1}$Department of Industrial Engineering, Ozyegin University, Istanbul, Turkiye\\[3pt]
\texttt{nur.percin@ozu.edu.tr, ali.ekici@ozyegin.edu.tr}
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
Research and development (R\&D) testing of refrigerators requires coordinating multiple products, heterogeneous test chambers, environmental constraints, and strict due dates. In practice, testing activities are commonly planned using fixed sample policies, although sample sizes directly affect both workload generation and opportunities for parallel execution. This study introduces the Test Chamber Scheduling with Sample Optimization Problem (TCSSOP), a novel scheduling problem in which sample sizes are treated as decision variables. The problem integrates chamber-environment assignment, resource-constrained scheduling, stage-dependent testing logic, and decision-dependent workload generation while satisfying temperature, humidity, and voltage compatibility constraints.

To solve TCSSOP, an iterative two-stage framework is proposed. The first stage assigns chamber environments according to workload distribution and chamber capabilities. The second stage optimizes sample sizes through local search and evaluates solutions using an earliest-due-date-based scheduling algorithm. To improve exploration, a Variable Neighborhood Search (VNS) diversification mechanism perturbs sample-size decisions and investigates alternative workload structures. The framework also incorporates comprehensive feasibility validation.

Computational experiments on 90 benchmark scenarios show that jointly optimizing sample sizes and chamber assignments substantially reduces total tardiness compared with the conventional fixed three-sample policy. The best-performing configuration, based on a \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS fallback strategy with \(K_{\max}=200\), consistently achieved the strongest overall performance. The proposed framework contributes to the scheduling literature by introducing a decision-dependent workload structure and provides a practical decision-support tool for improving testing-resource utilization and reducing project delays.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Test chamber scheduling; Sample size optimization; Decision-dependent workload; Resource-constrained scheduling; Variable neighborhood search; Multi-stage testing systems

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The refrigerator industry operates under intense global competition, compressed innovation cycles, and increasingly strict compliance regimes. Manufacturers are expected to introduce new models rapidly while simultaneously satisfying market-specific performance, safety, and energy-efficiency requirements \citep{IEA2021,IEC60335-1,IEC60335-2-24,IEC62552}. Recent operations-management evidence also highlights that launch-timing decisions are tightly linked to quality-assurance strategy and commercial outcomes, especially in settings where recall risk and time-to-market pressure interact \citep{KimKoenigsbergOfek2022}. Under these conditions, design approval testing becomes a strategic operational function: products cannot proceed to certification and commercialization unless all required tests are completed successfully and on time.

In practice, R\&D testing delays directly postpone production start and market entry, reducing responsiveness to demand and weakening competitive position in time-sensitive launch windows. At the same time, the testing function relies on capital-intensive infrastructure, including climate chambers, station equipment, instrumentation, and specialized personnel. OECD science and technology indicators report persistently high business-sector R\&D intensity and continued large-scale expenditure commitments in advanced economies, underscoring the financial pressure to use test capacity efficiently \citep{OECD_MSTI2024,OECD_MSTI2025}. Inefficient use of test capacity therefore creates a double penalty: longer lead times and lower return on fixed assets.

The resource side of the problem is also challenging. Test chambers, stations, instrumentation, and technical staff are costly and limited. A chamber may contain several stations, but those stations share a common temperature and humidity setting. Thus, capacity is not simply the total number of stations in the laboratory; it is the number of compatible stations available under the correct environmental and electrical conditions. This feature differentiates test-chamber planning from standard parallel-machine scheduling.

From an operations-research perspective, this setting combines multiple difficult planning layers: resource-constrained multi-project coordination, heterogeneous parallel-machine allocation, and strict feasibility constraints driven by industrial test protocols. Recent survey studies emphasize that practical scheduling systems increasingly require integrated treatment of precedence, resource heterogeneity, and operational constraints rather than isolated sequencing decisions \citep{HartmannBriskorn2022,GomezSanchezLallaRuizFernandezGilCastroVoss2023}. In related unrelated-parallel-machine environments, machine eligibility, delivery-time pressure, and tardiness-oriented objectives are known to significantly increase computational difficulty and motivate advanced heuristic designs \citep{UlagaDurasevicJakobovic2022,MaeckerShenMonch2023}.

The testing system considered in this study is motivated by refrigerator R\&D operations at Vestel. Products compete for a set of heterogeneous climate chambers, each containing several stations that operate under a common environmental configuration. Test operations require specific temperature and humidity conditions; some products also require voltage-compatible execution. In addition, the testing process follows stage-state logic: gas amount determination precedes pull-down testing, downstream tests are released after the pull-down phase, and final consumer-usage tests require the relevant downstream-stage release conditions to be satisfied.

A key operational feature is the use of physical samples. Industrial practice commonly applies a fixed three-sample policy. This rule is simple, but it ignores the fact that sample size is itself a scheduling decision. Pull-down tests scale with the number of samples and therefore increase upstream workload, whereas additional samples can enable downstream tests to run in parallel and reduce completion times. The effect of sample size on tardiness is consequently non-monotone and depends on due dates, chamber eligibility, environmental bottlenecks, and stage-state interactions.

The fixed-sample policy is attractive because it is easy to communicate and implement. However, it treats all products as if they had the same urgency, same downstream test structure, and same marginal benefit from parallelism. In reality, one product may benefit substantially from an additional sample because several downstream tests can then be processed simultaneously, while another product may gain little because its due date is loose or because compatible chambers are unavailable. Therefore, the marginal value of samples is project-specific and resource-dependent.

We define the resulting problem as the \emph{Test Chamber Scheduling with Sample Optimization Problem} (TCSSOP). TCSSOP combines heterogeneous parallel resources, chamber-level environmental assignment, humidity and voltage eligibility, multi-stage test logic, and decision-dependent workload generation. The objective is to minimize total tardiness, with total sample usage used as a secondary lexicographic criterion.

The proposed solution framework is designed around the feedback between sample decisions and chamber configuration. A change in sample sizes modifies the environment-specific workload distribution, especially through pull-down operations. This change may make the previous chamber assignment unsuitable. Conversely, a different chamber assignment may change which products benefit most from additional samples. For this reason, the framework alternates between chamber assignment and sample-size optimization rather than solving them once in isolation.

Another important practical feature is feasibility assurance. Industrial schedules must be implementable at the level of chambers, stations, products, and samples. A schedule that violates humidity compatibility, voltage feasibility, station non-overlap, sample non-overlap, or stage-state logic cannot be used operationally even if its objective value appears attractive. The proposed framework therefore includes explicit validation checks after schedule construction.

This study makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It benchmarks the standard fixed three-sample policy against endogenous sample-count optimization and quantifies its impact on total tardiness.
    \item It proposes an iterative two-stage integrated framework that captures assignment--scheduling feedback by reconfiguring chamber settings after sample decisions change.
    \item It isolates and reports the additional improvement delivered by VNS-based diversification beyond outer-iteration reconfiguration, including no-improvement fallback behavior.
\end{itemize}
The computational results show that these components jointly produce substantial tardiness reductions across 90 benchmark scenarios.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature_review} reviews related literature. Section~\ref{sec:pd} defines the problem formally and presents notation. Section~\ref{sec:solution} details the proposed solution approach. Section~\ref{sec:computational_study} reports computational findings, and Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes with managerial insights and future research directions.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

The problem is related to several scheduling and test-planning streams. Resource-constrained multi-project scheduling and unrelated parallel-machine scheduling provide the closest scheduling background because multiple projects compete for heterogeneous resources under precedence, due-date, and eligibility restrictions. Recent surveys emphasize that practical scheduling systems increasingly require integrated treatment of resource heterogeneity, precedence, and operational constraints \citep{HartmannBriskorn2022,GomezSanchezLallaRuizFernandezGilCastroVoss2023}. In unrelated parallel-machine environments, machine eligibility and tardiness-oriented objectives substantially increase computational difficulty and motivate specialized heuristics \citep{UlagaDurasevicJakobovic2022,MaeckerShenMonch2023}.

Resource-constrained multi-project scheduling provides a natural reference point because several projects are processed simultaneously and share limited resources. However, standard RCMPSP formulations usually assume that the set of activities and their processing requirements are known in advance. In TCSSOP, the activity set is partly induced by the sample vector. Pull-down operations are generated per sample, and downstream parallelism depends on the number of available physical units. This workload-generation feature is not captured by conventional multi-project scheduling models.

Unrelated parallel-machine scheduling is also relevant because chambers differ in capacity and technical eligibility. A station located in a voltage-capable chamber is not equivalent to a station in a non-voltage chamber when the product requires voltage-compatible testing. Similarly, humidity-controlled tests can be processed only in chambers with humidity adjustment. These eligibility restrictions interact with due dates and stage releases, making simple dispatching rules insufficient as a standalone solution.

Industrial scheduling often also involves setup or configuration decisions. The survey by \citet{Allahverdi2015} shows that ignoring setup-related effects can produce unrealistic schedules. In test-chamber systems, chamber environmental settings play a similar role because each chamber can serve only tests compatible with its current temperature-humidity configuration. This creates a reconfigurable resource-allocation problem related in spirit to reconfigurable manufacturing systems \citep{Koren1999,MehrabiUlsoyKoren2000}, although the present setting concerns testing rather than production.

Integrated decision-making models such as order acceptance and scheduling combine workload selection with sequencing under capacity limits \citep{Slotnick2011,WangYe2019}. Reliability demonstration testing optimizes sample size and test duration under statistical risk or cost criteria \citep{Fernandez2022}, but typically abstracts away detailed chamber capacity and stage-dependent scheduling. TCSSOP lies between these areas: sample sizes generate the workload, while the resulting operations must be scheduled on constrained heterogeneous chambers.

The reliability test-planning literature is particularly important because it recognizes sample size as a decision variable. Nevertheless, its main focus is generally statistical confidence, risk, cost, and test duration. The operational question of when and where each generated test should be executed is usually simplified. In industrial R\&D laboratories, however, a sample-size plan that is statistically or managerially reasonable may still be difficult to execute if it creates workload in a bottleneck environment or in voltage-capable chambers. TCSSOP therefore integrates sample-size planning with detailed resource feasibility.

Methodologically, VNS and related neighborhood-based metaheuristics have performed well in complex scheduling settings because they combine local improvement with systematic diversification \citep{MejiaYuraszeck2020}. The diversification mechanism proposed here differs from conventional VNS designs by perturbing sample-size decisions. Thus, the search explores alternative workload structures, which then induce different chamber-utilization and scheduling regimes.

The gap addressed in this paper is therefore not only the presence of additional constraints, but the endogenous nature of the workload itself. In classical scheduling, processing requirements are typically known before the schedule is constructed. In the present system, however, the number of physical samples selected for each product changes the number of pull-down operations and also changes how downstream tests can be distributed across samples. A fixed-job representation would miss this trade-off and would evaluate schedules under an artificially rigid workload. TCSSOP explicitly connects the test-planning decision and the resource-constrained scheduling decision within the same framework.

This distinction is important from both methodological and managerial perspectives. Methodologically, it creates a search space in which a move can improve the schedule even if it increases total processing time, because the same move may increase feasible parallelism. Managerially, it challenges the common rule that fewer samples always mean lower burden. In congested multi-stage testing systems, a carefully placed additional sample can reduce the completion time of a critical project enough to offset the extra pull-down effort. The proposed framework is designed to identify such cases.

\section{Problem Definition and Notation}
\label{sec:pd}

\subsection{Industrial problem setting}
\label{subsec:industrial_setting}

This study is motivated by a real-world refrigerator R\&D testing environment in which newly designed products must complete standardized laboratory tests before being released to the market. The facility consists of climate-controlled chambers, each containing multiple parallel testing stations. Stations within the same chamber operate under a common chamber-level environmental setting; therefore, all tests processed simultaneously in a chamber share the same temperature-humidity configuration.

A set of products \(\mathcal{P}\) must be processed in this environment. Each product corresponds to a development project with due date \(d_p\), reflecting market launch deadlines or internal planning targets. A product is completed only after all required tests have been executed, and its completion time \(C_p\) is the finish time of its last required operation.

Unlike classical scheduling environments, required tests are not identical for all products. Test requirements are determined jointly by climatic class, electrical specifications, optional engineering decisions, and market-specific energy regulations. Consequently, each product \(p\) is associated with a subset of tests \(\mathcal{T}_p\subseteq\mathcal{T}\). This product-dependent test structure creates heterogeneous workload patterns across projects and environments.

A critical feature of the system is the use of physical samples. Each product is represented by a number of identical units, and the sample count directly affects the generated workload. Pull-down tests are executed once for each sample, whereas most downstream tests are executed once per product but can be assigned to any available physical sample. Thus, increasing sample count raises upstream workload while potentially enabling downstream parallelism. This coupling between sample decisions, resource feasibility, and scheduling performance is the defining characteristic of TCSSOP.

\subsection{System description}
\label{subsec:system}

The facility contains a set of chambers \(\mathcal{C}\). Chamber \(c\) has \(M_c\) stations and supports temperature adjustment; only selected chambers support humidity adjustment \(h_c\) and voltage adjustment \(v_c\). Stations in the same chamber operate under one common environment \(X_c\), where an environment represents a temperature-humidity combination.

The set of products is denoted by \(\mathcal{P}\). Each product \(p\) has a due date \(d_p\), a voltage requirement indicator \(\nu_p\), and a product-specific required test set \(\mathcal{T}_p \subseteq \mathcal{T}\). Test requirements are determined by target market, climatic class, energy-efficiency regulation, optional engineering decisions, and electrical specification. Table~\ref{tab:test_attributes_short} summarizes the test catalog used in the study.

The chamber-level nature of environmental control is central to the problem. When a chamber is assigned to a temperature-humidity environment, all stations inside that chamber inherit the same condition. Therefore, the planner cannot independently assign different stations in the same chamber to different environments during the same configuration period. This creates a capacity-allocation problem at the chamber level and a scheduling problem at the station level.

Product due dates represent market launch deadlines or internal project milestones. The completion time of a product is the finish time of its last required test. Since tests may be performed on different samples and in different chambers, completion is not determined by a single route through the facility. Instead, it emerges from the interaction of release logic, chamber compatibility, station availability, and sample availability.

The chamber table highlights why TCSSOP cannot be reduced to a simple capacity-balancing problem. A workload peak at an ambient-temperature environment may be served by many chambers, but a workload peak involving humidity or voltage may be restricted to a much smaller subset. Therefore, the planning model must track both total station capacity and capability-specific station capacity.

The one-sided voltage rule is also important. Products requiring voltage-compatible execution can only be assigned to voltage-capable chambers. Products without such a requirement may be assigned to either voltage-capable or non-voltage-capable chambers, provided that environmental compatibility is satisfied. Consequently, assigning too much non-voltage work to voltage-capable chambers may indirectly harm voltage-sensitive products by occupying scarce eligible capacity.

\subsection{Test requirements driven by climatic class and target market}
\label{subsec:req_generation}

Product-specific test sets are generated from climatic class, energy regulation, optional engineering decisions, and voltage specification. In the considered industrial setting, test requirements are determined by the target market in which the product will be commercialized. Regulatory frameworks and environmental standards impose specific testing conditions that must be satisfied prior to product release. As a result, both the set of required tests and their execution environments are product-dependent.

A primary determinant of test requirements is the climatic class of the target market, which defines the ambient temperature conditions under which performance tests must be conducted. The benchmark logic considers Tropical, Subtropical, Normal, and Subnormal operating classes, which induce different temperature-dependent performance and energy-efficiency requirements. Energy-efficiency tests are additionally determined by market-specific regulations and may involve dual-point or single-point measurements at \(16^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\), or \(32^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Optional tests such as freezing capacity and temperature rise are included only when specified by the product engineer.

The environmental requirement of a test is represented at the required test-operation level. In notation, \(e(t)\) denotes the environment of a selected test variant \(t\). When a product-specific requirement selects among alternative temperatures, the selected variant is treated as the corresponding test type in the catalog. Thus, the scheduler sees a binary required-test matrix over concrete test variants, each with a fixed environment.

Voltage is modeled at the product level rather than the test level because the electrical configuration follows the product's target market. If a product requires voltage-compatible execution, all of its operations must be processed on voltage-capable chambers. This one-sided condition reflects practice: a voltage-capable chamber can process a product without voltage requirements, but the reverse is not feasible.

The test catalog contains several alternative variants. For example, energy-efficiency tests may be performed at \(16^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\), or \(32^\circ\mathrm{C}\), while performance tests can be required at different temperatures according to climatic class. These alternatives prevent the workload from being uniformly distributed over environments. In many instances, early-stage tests concentrate at high-temperature environments, while consumer-usage and optional tests create additional demand at \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and humidity-controlled environments. This uneven distribution is a main source of bottlenecks.

The target-market logic also implies that not all tests in the catalog are required simultaneously. Some tests are mandatory, some are alternatives, and some are optional. For example, one performance test may be selected from a climatic-class group, while another product may require a different performance-temperature condition. This mechanism creates product-level heterogeneity even within the same benchmark scenario.

\subsection{Decision-dependent workload}
\label{subsec:workload}

Each product has a sample-size decision \(s_p\), bounded by \(s_{\min} \le s_p \le s_{\max}\). Pull-down tests are executed once per sample, whereas other required tests are executed once per product. Thus, the multiplicity of test \(t\) for product \(p\) is
\[
m_{pt}(s_p)=
\begin{cases}
s_p, & \text{if }t\text{ is a pull-down test,}\\
1, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
For environment \(e\), the induced workload is
\[
W_e(S)=
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
\sum_{\substack{t\in\mathcal{T}_p\\e(t)=e}}
m_{pt}(s_p)\tau_t,
\]
where \(\tau_t\) is the processing time of test \(t\). Voltage-sensitive workload \(W_e^V(S)\) is defined similarly by summing only products with \(\nu_p=1\).

This formulation captures the central trade-off. Increasing \(s_p\) increases pull-down workload but may reduce tardiness by enabling downstream parallelism. Reducing \(s_p\) saves workload but may serialize downstream testing on too few physical units.

\subsection{Stage-state logic and sample feasibility}
\label{subsec:stage_logic}

The scheduling logic is implemented through four job categories: gas amount determination, pull-down, other downstream tests, and consumer-usage tests. Gas amount determination is executed at most once for each product when it is required by the product's test matrix. If it is required, it is the first operation of the product and is released at time zero. If it is not required, the product is treated as already released for the pull-down/downstream logic.

Pull-down is executed once for each physical sample of a product. For product \(p\) with \(s_p\) samples, the pull-down phase therefore generates \(s_p\) sample-specific operations. Each pull-down operation can start only after the gas amount determination operation of the same product has finished. Different pull-down operations of the same product may run in parallel on different samples and compatible stations, subject to station availability and sample-level non-overlap.

The downstream non-consumer tests consist of the required energy-efficiency, performance, freezing-capacity, and temperature-rise tests. In the implemented configuration, these tests are released only after the full pull-down phase of the product has been completed, that is, after all sample-level pull-down operations have finished. Each required downstream non-consumer test is scheduled once per product and may be assigned to any available sample of that product after this release time.

Consumer-usage tests are modeled as the final category. A required consumer-usage test is released only after the pull-down phase has been completed and after all required downstream non-consumer tests of the same product have been started. Thus, the release time for consumer-usage tests is the maximum of the pull-down phase completion time and the latest start time among the required downstream non-consumer tests. Consumer-usage tests do not wait for those downstream non-consumer tests to finish.

Let \(G_p\) be the gas operation of product \(p\), \(PD_{pj}\) be the pull-down operation of sample \(j\), and \(\mathcal{O}_p\) and \(\mathcal{U}_p\) be the sets of required downstream non-consumer and consumer-usage tests, respectively. If gas and pull-down are required, then
\[
G_p \prec PD_{pj} \qquad \forall j=1,\ldots,s_p.
\]
The implemented downstream release rule is
\[
\max_{j=1,\ldots,s_p} C_{PD_{pj}} \le S_o
\qquad \forall o\in\mathcal{O}_p,
\]
where \(S_o\) denotes the start time of downstream non-consumer test \(o\). For each consumer-usage test \(u\in\mathcal{U}_p\),
\[
S_u \ge
\max\left\{
\max_{j=1,\ldots,s_p} C_{PD_{pj}},
\max_{o\in\mathcal{O}_p} S_o
\right\}.
\]
The second term is based on the start times of the downstream non-consumer tests, not their completion times. Sample feasibility is enforced independently from product-stage feasibility: at any time, a physical sample can process at most one operation.

Sample availability is the mechanism through which additional samples create parallelism. If a product has only the minimum number of samples, several downstream tests may need to wait for the same units even when compatible chamber stations are idle. If additional samples are available, downstream tests can be distributed over more physical units after release. The benefit depends on the timing of release, due-date urgency, and whether compatible chambers are available in the required environments. This is why sample optimization is integrated with chamber assignment and scheduling rather than solved as an isolated planning calculation.

\subsection{Feasibility conditions and objective}
\label{subsec:objective}

A feasible schedule must include all and only the required tests with the multiplicities induced by \(S\). Each operation must be assigned to a chamber whose environment matches the test requirement; humidity-controlled tests can be assigned only to chambers with \(h_c=1\), and if \(\nu_p=1\), all operations of product \(p\) must be assigned only to chambers with \(v_c=1\). In addition, each station can process at most one operation at a time, each physical sample can be used by at most one operation at a time, stage-state logic must be satisfied, and the completion time of each product is defined as the finish time of its last required operation.

Let \(C_p\) be the completion time of product \(p\). Product tardiness is
\[
T_p=\max(0,C_p-d_p),
\]
and the primary objective is
\[
\min T^{tot}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}T_p.
\]
Among solutions with equal total tardiness, the solution with smaller total sample usage
\[
S^{tot}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}s_p
\]
is preferred. The evaluation tuple is therefore \(Eval=(T^{tot},S^{tot})\) under lexicographic ordering.

\subsection{Mathematical formulation}
\label{subsec:math_formulation}

The following expanded mixed-integer formulation defines TCSSOP formally. It is not used as the computational solution method for the large benchmark instances; instead, it clarifies the complete decision structure that the heuristic approximates. The expansion uses the maximum sample index set \(\mathcal{J}=\{1,\ldots,s_{\max}\}\), so the operation set is fixed before optimization and sample-size decisions activate only the required subset of sample-specific operations.

Let \(r_{pt}\) be 1 if product \(p\) requires test \(t\), and 0 otherwise. Let \(g\) denote gas amount determination and \(pd\) denote pull-down. Let \(\mathcal{T}^{D}\) be the set of downstream non-consumer tests and \(\mathcal{T}^{U}\) be the set of consumer-usage tests. Candidate operations are defined as \(G_p\) for gas amount determination, \(PD_{pj}\) for pull-down of sample \(j\), and \(O_{ptj}\) for assigning downstream or consumer test \(t\) of product \(p\) to sample \(j\). The set of all candidate operations is denoted by \(\mathcal{O}^{\max}\). For each operation \(o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max}\), let \(p(o)\), \(e(o)\), and \(\tau_o\) denote its product, required environment, and processing time. The binary variable \(a_{pj}\) activates sample \(j\) of product \(p\), \(b_o\) activates operation \(o\), \(x_{ce}\) assigns chamber \(c\) to environment \(e\), and \(y_{ocr}\) assigns operation \(o\) to station \(r\) of chamber \(c\), where \(r\in\mathcal{R}_c\). Start and completion times are \(S_o\) and \(C_o\). Product completion and tardiness are \(C_p\) and \(T_p\). A sufficiently large constant is denoted by \(B\).

Sample activation and sample-size bounds are
\begin{align}
& s_{\min}\le \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}a_{pj}\le s_{\max} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\\
& a_{pj}\le a_{p,j-1} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ j=2,\ldots,s_{\max}.
\end{align}
The second constraint activates samples consecutively and removes symmetric sample labels.

The operation-activation constraints are
\begin{align}
& b_{G_p}=r_{pg} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\\
& b_{PD_{pj}}=r_{p,pd}a_{pj} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}} b_{O_{ptj}}=r_{pt} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{D}\cup\mathcal{T}^{U},\\
& b_{O_{ptj}}\le a_{pj} && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{D}\cup\mathcal{T}^{U},\ j\in\mathcal{J}.
\end{align}
Thus, pull-down is generated once for every active sample, whereas each required downstream or consumer test is performed once and assigned to one active sample.

Chamber-environment assignment and operation assignment are enforced by
\begin{align}
& \sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}x_{ce}=1 && \forall c\in\mathcal{C},\\
& \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_c}y_{ocr}=b_o && \forall o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max},\\
& y_{ocr}\le x_{c,e(o)} && \forall o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max},\ c\in\mathcal{C},\ r\in\mathcal{R}_c,\\
& y_{ocr}\le h_c && \forall o:e(o)\in\mathcal{E}^{H},\ c\in\mathcal{C},\ r\in\mathcal{R}_c,\\
& y_{ocr}\le v_c && \forall o:\nu_{p(o)}=1,\ c\in\mathcal{C},\ r\in\mathcal{R}_c.
\end{align}
These constraints ensure that every active operation is assigned to exactly one compatible station, with humidity and voltage restrictions enforced at the chamber level.

Processing times are represented as
\begin{align}
& 0\le S_o\le Bb_o,\qquad 0\le C_o\le Bb_o && \forall o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max},\\
& C_o\ge S_o+\tau_o-B(1-b_o) && \forall o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max},\\
& C_o\le S_o+\tau_o+B(1-b_o) && \forall o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max}.
\end{align}

Station non-overlap is modeled with binary sequencing variables \(\delta_{oo'cr}\), where \(o<o'\):
\begin{align}
& S_{o'}\ge C_o-B(3-y_{ocr}-y_{o'cr}-\delta_{oo'cr})
&& \forall o<o',\ c\in\mathcal{C},\ r\in\mathcal{R}_c,\\
& S_o\ge C_{o'}-B(2-y_{ocr}-y_{o'cr}+\delta_{oo'cr})
&& \forall o<o',\ c\in\mathcal{C},\ r\in\mathcal{R}_c.
\end{align}
Sample non-overlap is enforced similarly. Let \(\mathcal{O}^{\max}_{pj}\) be the candidate operations of product \(p\) that use physical sample \(j\). For \(o<o'\) in \(\mathcal{O}^{\max}_{pj}\), binary variable \(\gamma_{oo'}\) determines their order:
\begin{align}
& S_{o'}\ge C_o-B(3-b_o-b_{o'}-\gamma_{oo'})
&& \forall p,\ j,\ o<o'\in\mathcal{O}^{\max}_{pj},\\
& S_o\ge C_{o'}-B(2-b_o-b_{o'}+\gamma_{oo'})
&& \forall p,\ j,\ o<o'\in\mathcal{O}^{\max}_{pj}.
\end{align}

Stage-state logic is imposed as follows. Let \(F_p^{PD}\) denote the completion time of the pull-down phase and let \(A_p^D\) denote the latest start time among required downstream non-consumer tests:
\begin{align}
& S_{PD_{pj}}\ge C_{G_p}-B(1-b_{PD_{pj}})
&& \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& F_p^{PD}\ge C_{PD_{pj}}
&& \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& S_{O_{ptj}}\ge F_p^{PD}-B(1-b_{O_{ptj}})
&& \forall p,\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{D},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& A_p^D\ge S_{O_{ptj}}-B(1-b_{O_{ptj}})
&& \forall p,\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{D},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& S_{O_{ptj}}\ge F_p^{PD}-B(1-b_{O_{ptj}})
&& \forall p,\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{U},\ j\in\mathcal{J},\\
& S_{O_{ptj}}\ge A_p^D-B(1-b_{O_{ptj}})
&& \forall p,\ t\in\mathcal{T}^{U},\ j\in\mathcal{J}.
\end{align}
These constraints reproduce the implemented logic: gas precedes pull-down, downstream non-consumer tests wait for all pull-down operations to finish, and consumer-usage tests wait for pull-down completion and for all downstream non-consumer tests to have started.

Completion and tardiness are defined by
\begin{align}
& C_p\ge C_o && \forall p\in\mathcal{P},\ o\in\mathcal{O}^{\max}:p(o)=p,\\
& T_p\ge C_p-d_p,\qquad T_p\ge 0 && \forall p\in\mathcal{P}.
\end{align}
The variable domains are
\[
a_{pj},b_o,x_{ce},y_{ocr},\delta_{oo'cr},\gamma_{oo'}\in\{0,1\},
\qquad
S_o,C_o,F_p^{PD},A_p^D,C_p,T_p\ge 0.
\]
The lexicographic objective is
\[
\min_{\mathrm{lex}}
\left(
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}T_p,
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}a_{pj}
\right).
\]
The first component minimizes total tardiness, while the second selects the smaller total sample count among equal-tardiness schedules.

\section{Solution Methodology}
\label{sec:solution}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:framework}

The proposed solution framework coordinates three decision layers: chamber-environment assignment, sample-size optimization, and feasible scheduling. The method starts from the fixed three-sample policy. At each outer iteration, chamber settings are selected for the current sample vector, sample counts are improved under the fixed chamber assignment, and the resulting solution is evaluated by a constructive scheduler. If local improvement stagnates, a VNS diversification phase perturbs sample sizes and re-applies local improvement.

The framework is deliberately hierarchical. Chamber assignment is treated as a configuration decision, sample-size optimization as a workload-generation decision, and scheduling as the exact feasibility and performance evaluation layer. This hierarchy keeps the search computationally tractable while still allowing feedback between layers. A sample vector is never evaluated purely by workload volume; it is ultimately decoded into a resource-feasible schedule.

The outer loop is necessary because sample-size changes alter the workload distribution used by the chamber-assignment model. If chamber settings were fixed after the first assignment, later sample decisions would be evaluated under a potentially outdated capacity distribution. Conversely, reassigning chambers without changing sample sizes would ignore the main source of decision-dependent workload. The iterative structure therefore captures the two-way interaction between resource configuration and workload generation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Integrated TCSSOP heuristic}
\label{alg:integrated_short}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize \(S^{current}\) with \(s_p=3\) for all products.
\State Set incumbent \((X^{best},S^{best},\Pi^{best},Eval^{best})\leftarrow(\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset,(+\infty,+\infty))\).
\For{\(iter=1,\ldots,I_{\max}\)}
    \State Assign chamber environments \(X^{current}\) for \(S^{current}\).
    \State Apply sample-size local search under \(X^{current}\), obtaining \((S^{local},\Pi^{local},Eval^{local})\).
    \If{\(Eval^{local}\) improves \(Eval^{best}\)}
        \State Update incumbent and continue with \(S^{current}\leftarrow S^{local}\).
    \Else
        \State Apply VNS diversification to \(S^{best}\) under \(X^{best}\).
        \If{VNS improves the incumbent}
            \State Update incumbent and continue.
        \Else
            \State Terminate.
        \EndIf
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return incumbent solution.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Chamber assignment}
\label{subsec:chamber_assignment}

For a fixed sample vector \(S\), chamber environments are determined from environment-specific workload shares. Let
\[
W^{tot}(S)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}W_e(S),
\qquad
W^{V,tot}(S)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}W_e^V(S).
\]
The total workload share is
\[
\phi_e=\frac{W_e(S)}{W^{tot}(S)}.
\]
For voltage-sensitive workload, if \(W^{V,tot}(S)>0\),
\[
\phi_e^V=\frac{W_e^V(S)}{W^{V,tot}(S)}.
\]
Otherwise, \(\phi_e^V=0\) for all \(e\in\mathcal{E}\).

Let
\[
M^{tot}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_c,
\qquad
M^{V,tot}=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_cv_c
\]
denote the total station capacity and the station capacity located in voltage-capable chambers, respectively. Total workload targets are scaled by \(M^{tot}\), whereas voltage-sensitive workload targets are scaled only by \(M^{V,tot}\), since voltage-sensitive products can be processed only in voltage-capable chambers. Therefore,
\[
T_e=\phi_e M^{tot},
\qquad
T_e^V=\phi_e^V M^{V,tot}.
\]
Let
\[
\mathcal{E}^V(S)=\{e\in\mathcal{E}: W_e^V(S)>0\}
\]
be the set of environments with positive voltage-sensitive workload.

The assignment model uses proportional workload targets rather than absolute horizon-based capacity requirements. Let \(x_{ce}=1\) if chamber \(c\) is assigned to environment \(e\), and let \(\mathcal{E}^H\) be the set of humidity-controlled environments. The exact assignment model minimizes absolute deviations from workload-based target capacities:
\begin{align}
\min\quad & \sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}z_e+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}z_e^V \\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}x_{ce}=1 && \forall c\in\mathcal{C},\\
& \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}x_{ce}\ge 1 && \forall e\in\mathcal{E},\\
& \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}v_cx_{ce}\ge 1 && \forall e\in\mathcal{E}^V(S),\\
& x_{ce}\le h_c && \forall c\in\mathcal{C},\ e\in\mathcal{E}^H,\\
& z_e\ge \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_cx_{ce}-T_e && \forall e\in\mathcal{E},\\
& z_e\ge T_e-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_cx_{ce} && \forall e\in\mathcal{E},\\
& z_e^V\ge \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_cv_cx_{ce}-T_e^V && \forall e\in\mathcal{E},\\
& z_e^V\ge T_e^V-\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}M_cv_cx_{ce} && \forall e\in\mathcal{E},\\
& x_{ce}\in\{0,1\},\quad z_e,z_e^V\ge 0 && \forall c,e.
\end{align}
The resulting assignment is then refined by swap and move neighborhoods. A candidate is retained only if it preserves environment coverage, humidity feasibility, and voltage feasibility. Feasible candidates are evaluated with the lightweight EDD evaluation mode described in Section~\ref{subsec:scheduling}, and a first-improvement rule is used.

The exact assignment model alone cannot represent all detailed scheduling interactions. For example, two chambers may have the same number of stations but different voltage or humidity capabilities. Similarly, the same aggregate capacity in an environment can perform differently depending on which physical chambers provide that capacity. The local refinement phase addresses these effects by exploring nearby chamber-environment mappings after the exact model produces a balanced initial configuration.

The exact model provides a balanced starting point, while the local refinement step incorporates scheduling-aware information that is not represented in the aggregate workload objective. In particular, two assignments with similar environment-level capacity deviations can produce different completion times because the detailed scheduler also sees due dates, release times, and sample conflicts. The local phase therefore acts as a bridge between aggregate chamber planning and detailed operational scheduling.

\subsection{Sample-size local search}
\label{subsec:sample_optimization}

Given chamber assignment \(X\), local search explores product-level sample changes. For each product \(p\), the neighborhood is
\[
\Delta_p \subseteq \{-2,-1,+1,+2\}
\]
subject to sample bounds. Candidate vectors are screened by the lightweight mode of the same EDD scheduler. If a move improves the lexicographic objective, the move is accepted and the current solution is updated.

Both increasing and decreasing sample counts are considered. Increasing a sample count may improve a tardy product by enabling more downstream parallelism. Decreasing a sample count may reduce congestion caused by pull-down operations and may also reduce the secondary objective when tardiness is unchanged.

A Late--Early Rebalance operator is also used. Products with positive tardiness are paired with products having earliness slack. For each pair, one sample is added to the late product and one sample is removed from the early product, subject to bounds. The paired move is accepted if total tardiness does not worsen and, under equal tardiness, total sample usage does not exceed the sample usage at the entry of the current local-search call.

The rebalance operator is designed to capture coordinated moves that a single-product neighborhood may miss. A late product may need one additional sample, but adding that sample alone increases total pull-down workload. Pairing it with a reduction on an early product can preserve or reduce total sample usage while redistributing parallelization potential toward more urgent projects.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Sample-size local search}
\label{alg:sample_ls_short}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Chamber assignment \(X\), sample vector \(S\)
\State Clamp all sample values to \([s_{\min},s_{\max}]\).
\State Construct an initial schedule evaluation \(Eval\).
\Repeat
    \State \(improved\leftarrow false\)
    \For{each product \(p\in\mathcal{P}\)}
        \State Define feasible moves \(\Delta_p\subseteq\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}\).
        \State Screen all \(s_p+\delta\), \(\delta\in\Delta_p\), using lightweight EDD evaluation.
        \If{the best screened move improves the incumbent}
            \State Apply the move and update \(Eval\).
            \State \(improved\leftarrow true\)
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Apply the Late--Early Rebalance operator.
    \If{the rebalance solution is accepted}
        \State Update \(S\) and \(Eval\); set \(improved\leftarrow true\).
    \EndIf
\Until{\(improved=false\) or the pass limit is reached}
\State \Return \((S,Eval)\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The lightweight evaluation is used for screening. Accepted outer-loop solutions are re-evaluated in schedule-building mode, which produces the full schedule and enables the validation checks. This two-level output strategy reduces computational effort while preserving consistency in final objective comparisons, because both modes use the same deterministic EDD decoder.

\subsection{Scheduling, lightweight evaluation, and feasibility validation}
\label{subsec:scheduling}

For fixed \(X\) and \(S\), schedules are constructed using an earliest-due-date (EDD) ready-set rule. Candidate jobs are generated project-wise from the current stage state, sample availability, and earliest feasible station time. The best ready job of each product enters a global candidate pool, and the next job is selected by due date, with earliest feasible start time as a tie-breaker.

Each selected job is assigned to the earliest compatible chamber station. Compatibility requires matching environment, humidity capability when needed, and voltage capability for voltage-sensitive products. Sample availability is updated after every assignment to prevent overlap on the same physical unit.

The fast schedule-aware scoring step is not an aggregate workload proxy and it does not use a different dispatching rule from the final scheduler. It is a lightweight execution mode of the same EDD-based constructive scheduler. For a fixed chamber assignment \(X\) and sample vector \(S\), the scheduler constructs the same ready-set sequence, applies the same chamber, humidity, voltage, station, sample, and stage-state feasibility rules, and returns the resulting total tardiness value. The computational saving comes from suppressing outputs that are not needed during neighborhood screening: the fast mode does not build the detailed operation-level schedule list or, when project-level lateness information is not needed, the project-result list.

More specifically, the score of a candidate is
\[
Score(X,S)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\max(0,C_p(X,S)-d_p),
\]
where \(C_p(X,S)\) is obtained from the same deterministic scheduler used for the final evaluation. One lightweight evaluation mode returns only total tardiness and is used when screening room-assignment moves, single-product sample-size moves, and VNS shake candidates. A second lightweight mode returns total tardiness and project-level completion/lateness values but still skips the operation-level schedule list; this mode is used when the Late--Early Rebalance operator requires project-level lateness and earliness information. Accepted outer-loop solutions are then re-evaluated in the schedule-building mode, which constructs the full operation-level schedule and enables the independent feasibility validation checks.

After construction, the schedule is validated independently. The validation checks required-test multiplicity, chamber-environment compatibility, humidity and voltage feasibility, station non-overlap, sample non-overlap, stage-state logic, and completion-time calculation. This layer is essential because infeasible schedules are operationally unusable regardless of objective value.

The validation layer also protects the higher-level search from hidden feasibility errors. Since local search and VNS generate many sample vectors, it is possible for a candidate to look promising under a fast score but fail detailed operational checks. By validating the full schedule after construction, the framework ensures that reported objective values correspond only to implementable schedules.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{EDD-based schedule construction}
\label{alg:scheduler_short}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Chamber assignment \(X\), sample vector \(S\)
\State Initialize station availability, sample availability, product states, and schedule \(\Pi\).
\While{at least one product has an executable operation}
    \State Create an empty candidate pool.
    \For{each product \(p\in\mathcal{P}\)}
        \State Identify the best ready operation of \(p\) using stage state, compatible chambers, and sample availability.
        \If{a ready operation exists}
            \State Add it to the candidate pool.
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State Select the next operation by earliest due date; break ties by earliest feasible start.
    \State Assign the operation to the earliest feasible compatible station and sample.
    \State Update station, sample, and product-stage states.
\EndWhile
\State Compute \(C_p\), \(T_p\), \(T^{tot}\), and \(S^{tot}\).
\State Validate all feasibility conditions.
\State \Return \((\Pi,(T^{tot},S^{tot}))\)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The scheduler is deterministic under fixed inputs and tie-breaking rules. This determinism is useful in the computational study because performance differences can be attributed to algorithmic design rather than random variation. It also makes the local-search and VNS phases easier to compare, since the same sample vector and chamber assignment always decode into the same schedule.

\subsection{VNS diversification}
\label{subsec:vns}

VNS is activated when the outer loop does not improve the incumbent. The method uses a fixed fallback ratio sequence
\[
R^{VNS}=[0.20,0.30].
\]
For each ratio, up to \(K_{\max}\) shake attempts are performed. A shake selects the corresponding fraction of products uniformly without replacement and alternates between two directions: \textsc{DOWN}, which resets selected samples to \(s_{\min}\), and \textsc{UP}, which resets selected samples to the VNS upper level. The perturbed vector is then improved by the same sample-size local search. The first locally improved candidate that improves the incumbent is accepted.

This design changes the workload structure rather than only the sequence of existing operations. As a result, VNS can escape local optima created by the current balance between pull-down workload, downstream parallelism, and chamber eligibility.

Each fallback ratio phase starts from the incumbent supplied by the outer framework. Within a ratio phase, each shake starts from the current VNS-best baseline, so an improving shake can become the baseline for later shakes in the same phase. This prevents drift through a chain of non-improving perturbations while still allowing improvement inside a phase to be exploited.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{VNS with fixed fallback ratios}
\label{alg:vns_short}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Chamber assignment \(X\), incumbent \((S^{inc},\Pi^{inc},Eval^{inc})\)
\State Set \(R^{VNS}=[0.20,0.30]\).
\For{each ratio \(r\in R^{VNS}\)}
    \State \(S^{vbest}\leftarrow S^{inc}\), \(Eval^{vbest}\leftarrow Eval^{inc}\).
    \For{\(k=1,\ldots,K_{\max}\)}
        \State \(S^{cand}\leftarrow S^{vbest}\).
        \If{\(k\) is odd}
            \State Reset a uniformly selected \(r\)-fraction of eligible products to \(s_{\min}\).
        \Else
            \State Reset a uniformly selected \(r\)-fraction of eligible products to \(s_{\max VNS}\).
        \EndIf
        \State Apply sample-size local search to \(S^{cand}\) under \(X\).
        \If{the locally improved candidate improves \(Eval^{vbest}\)}
            \State Update \(S^{vbest}\) and \(Eval^{vbest}\).
        \EndIf
        \If{the locally improved candidate improves \(Eval^{inc}\)}
            \State \Return the candidate and set \(improved=true\).
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return the incumbent and set \(improved=false\).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The fallback structure avoids committing the search to a single perturbation intensity. A \(0.20\) perturbation often preserves useful sample-allocation structure while moving the search outside the immediate local basin. If this is insufficient, the \(0.30\) perturbation provides a stronger change. Larger perturbations were not selected for the final configuration because preliminary tests indicated that they often destroyed beneficial workload patterns generated by the local search.

\subsection{Integrated interpretation}
\label{subsec:integrated_interpretation}

The algorithmic components operate as a tightly coupled hierarchical framework. The outer solution framework governs acceptance decisions and termination; the chamber-assignment component determines feasible and refined chamber configurations; the sample-size local search optimizes workload generation under fixed chamber settings; the EDD-based scheduler constructs feasible schedules and provides objective evaluation; and the VNS component handles stagnation through controlled sample-size perturbations.

The integration of these components explicitly captures the interdependence between workload generation, resource configuration, and scheduling performance. Sample-size decisions define the workload structure of the system, chamber assignments determine how this workload is distributed across environment-dependent resources, and the scheduling procedure translates these decisions into operational performance under strict feasibility constraints.

A key feature of the proposed framework is the feedback loop between these decision layers. Changes in sample sizes alter workload distribution, which in turn affects chamber utilization and scheduling feasibility. This feedback is handled through iterative coordination between chamber assignment and sample-size optimization rather than by treating them as independent subproblems.

Furthermore, diversification through VNS operates directly on workload-defining parameters rather than sequencing decisions. This distinguishes the proposed approach from classical VNS-based scheduling methods and enables the search to explore substantially different workload structures while maintaining feasibility through subsequent local improvement and exact schedule validation.

\section{Computational Study}
\label{sec:computational_study}

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{subsec:exp_design}

The benchmark set contains 90 scenario combinations generated from \(10\) test matrices, \(3\) due-date scenarios, and \(3\) voltage scenarios. Each test matrix contains 50 product projects. Hence, each benchmark instance contains 50 projects, and the full experimental design is
\[
10 \times 3 \times 3 = 90
\]
instances. The test matrices define the binary required-test vector of each project over the catalog in Table~\ref{tab:test_attributes_short}. The due-date and voltage scenarios are then crossed with these test matrices to separate the effects of portfolio composition, deadline pressure, and voltage-compatibility intensity.

The instances are synthetically generated using ranges and structural rules calibrated from industrial practice, allowing controlled experiments without relying on proprietary raw data. The use of structured synthetic instances provides two advantages. First, it preserves confidentiality because no proprietary project-level dataset is disclosed. Second, it allows the experimental factors to be varied systematically. By combining due-date regimes, voltage-intensity regimes, and test-matrix regimes, the benchmark isolates how different operational pressures affect the value of sample-size optimization.

The due-date scenarios differ in both location and dispersion. Scenario~1 is a relatively concentrated deadline setting, with due dates ranging from 42 to 57 days and a median of 50 days; its first and third quartiles are 46 and 54 days, respectively. Scenario~2 introduces a wider spread and earlier deadlines, with due dates ranging from 30 to 69 days, a median of 49.5 days, and first and third quartiles of 40 and 59 days. Scenario~3 represents a more relaxed but still heterogeneous deadline setting, with due dates ranging from 34 to 69 days, a median of 53 days, and first and third quartiles of 43 and 63 days.

Test matrices represent different product portfolios and required-test combinations. They mainly affect workload distribution across environments because gas and pull-down tests concentrate upstream demand, while energy, performance, freezing, temperature-rise, and consumer-usage tests distribute downstream workload across several temperatures. Therefore, the 90-scenario benchmark combines deadline tightness, resource eligibility pressure, and environment-dependent workload imbalance without relying on external graphics.

The test matrices are especially important because they create different environment-level bottlenecks. Some matrices place more downstream work at \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\), while others increase demand at low-temperature or high-temperature environments. Since chamber assignments are made at the environment level, these workload shifts directly affect the usefulness of reassignment and the value of additional samples.

The instances are provided to the Java implementation as a structured input table with one row per project. Each row specifies the instance identifier, project identifier, test-matrix scenario, due-date scenario, voltage scenario, due date, voltage requirement indicator, and the binary required-test columns. The implementation was developed and executed in Java using the Eclipse IDE.

\subsection{Compared solution variants}
\label{subsec:methods_params}

Four variants are compared:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FS (Fixed Sample):} all products use \(s_p=3\); chamber assignment is performed once and no sample optimization is applied.
    \item \textbf{SO-noOuter:} sample sizes are optimized under the initial chamber assignment, without iterative chamber reassignment.
    \item \textbf{SO-noVNS:} sample optimization and chamber reassignment are iterated, but VNS diversification is disabled.
    \item \textbf{Proposed Framework:} sample optimization, chamber reassignment, and VNS diversification are all enabled.
\end{itemize}

All algorithmic variants were implemented in Java and executed from the Eclipse IDE under the same parameter settings. The chamber-environment assignment model was solved by an in-house exact branch-and-bound routine over chamber types, and the resulting chamber assignments were evaluated by the constructive scheduler described in Section~\ref{subsec:scheduling}. No instance-specific parameter tuning was applied.

\subsection{Parameter settings}
\label{subsec:parameter_settings}

To ensure a fair, reproducible, and unbiased comparison across all solution variants, a common parameterization protocol was adopted. Parameter values were calibrated only once during preliminary pilot runs and subsequently fixed for the entire computational study. No instance-specific tuning was performed. Consequently, performance differences among the competing methods can be attributed to algorithmic design rather than parameter adjustment.

All parameters are fixed across instances. The initial sample count is \(s_p^{(0)}=3\), \(s_{\min}=3\), \(s_{\max}=11\), and the VNS \textsc{UP} level is \(s_{\max VNS}=8\). The sample neighborhood is \(\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}\), the VNS fallback ratios are \([0.20,0.30]\), \(I_{\max}=100\), \(K_{\max}=200\), the sample local-search pass limit is \(50\), and the Late--Early Rebalance pair limit is \(10\) for the selected configuration. The objective is evaluated lexicographically as \((T^{tot},S^{tot})\).

The lower sample bound corresponds to the industrial baseline and prevents the optimization procedure from selecting sample counts below the minimum accepted engineering policy. The upper bounds allow the search to explore additional parallelism but prevent unrealistic prototype expansion. The separate \(s_{\max VNS}\) parameter limits the intensity of upward VNS perturbations while still allowing local search to refine sample counts within the broader feasible range.

The neighborhood \(\{-2,-1,+1,+2\}\) balances intensification and exploration. Unit moves provide fine adjustment, while two-unit moves allow the search to cross small local barriers caused by the discrete effect of sample availability. The same local-search structure is used across variants so that performance differences can be attributed to the presence or absence of outer reconfiguration and VNS diversification.

\subsection{Performance measures}
\label{subsec:metrics}

For method \(m\) and instance \(i\), the percentage gap is computed from total tardiness:
\[
Gap_{im}=
\frac{T^{tot}_{im}-T^{tot,best}_{i}}
{T^{tot,best}_{i}}\times 100,
\]
where \(T^{tot,best}_{i}\) is the best total tardiness observed for that instance in the relevant comparison. In the reported experiments, all denominators are positive. Best-instance counts are based on the lexicographic objective, so total sample usage breaks ties when total tardiness is equal.

Runtime is reported as a separate performance indicator rather than being included in the objective. This is appropriate because the primary planning objective is tactical schedule quality, while runtime reflects computational effort. The selected configuration is therefore not expected to be the fastest method; it is intended to provide the best tardiness performance under a reasonable offline planning setting. All experiments were run under the same computational environment and deterministic algorithmic settings; therefore, runtimes should be interpreted primarily as comparative indicators among the tested variants.

\subsection{Managerial implications}
\label{subsec:managerial}

The results provide three managerial insights. First, sample counts should be optimized rather than fixed. The value of an additional sample depends on due-date urgency, downstream parallelization potential, and the compatible chamber set. Second, sample optimization and chamber assignment should be updated together because changing sample counts shifts environment-specific and voltage-sensitive workload. Third, diversification is important in tactical planning: local sample changes alone may not escape a poor workload structure, whereas controlled VNS perturbations can reveal substantially better sample allocations.

The results also suggest that capacity shortages should not automatically be addressed by uniformly increasing prototype counts. Additional samples create both benefits and costs. They increase pull-down workload, but they can also shorten downstream completion times by enabling parallel tests. The useful managerial question is therefore not whether more samples are always better, but where additional samples create the largest reduction in tardiness.

Another implication concerns chamber configuration review. In many laboratories, chamber settings are planned periodically and then held fixed. The findings indicate that sample-allocation changes should trigger a reassessment of chamber environments, especially when workload shifts toward humidity-controlled or voltage-sensitive environments. This is particularly important in high voltage-intensity scenarios, where the set of eligible chambers is limited.

Finally, feasibility validation should be treated as a core part of decision support. A schedule that appears efficient at an aggregate level may fail because of sample overlap, station overlap, or chamber compatibility. The proposed framework separates lightweight evaluation from exact schedule construction and validation, allowing the search to remain efficient while ensuring that reported schedules are operationally meaningful.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

This study introduced TCSSOP, an integrated test-chamber scheduling problem with decision-dependent workload generation. The problem arises in refrigerator R\&D testing, where heterogeneous chambers, environmental compatibility, voltage and humidity restrictions, stage-state logic, due dates, and physical sample availability interact.

The proposed framework jointly optimizes chamber settings, sample sizes, and feasible schedules. Chamber environments are assigned using workload-balancing logic; sample sizes are improved by local search and Late--Early Rebalance; schedules are constructed by an EDD-based feasible scheduler; and VNS diversification perturbs sample decisions when local improvement stagnates. Feasibility is enforced through explicit checks for required tests, chamber compatibility, humidity and voltage eligibility, station and sample non-overlap, and stage-state correctness.

Computational experiments on 90 benchmark scenarios show that the selected proposed configuration with \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS fallback and \(K_{\max}=200\) consistently outperforms fixed-sample and ablated variants. The fixed three-sample policy is fast but produces substantially higher tardiness. The ablation results show that both iterative chamber reconfiguration and VNS diversification materially improve solution quality.

The main practical conclusion is that prototype sample allocation should be treated as a tactical planning variable. Samples should be increased selectively where additional parallelism reduces tardiness, and chamber configurations should be reassessed after sample decisions change. Future work may incorporate uncertain test durations, test failures and retesting, chamber setup times, explicit prototype-cost budgets, energy and labor objectives, and rolling-horizon project arrivals.

Several research extensions are natural. First, uncertainty can be incorporated through stochastic or robust scheduling models. Refrigerator tests may fail, require repetition, or take longer than planned due to stabilization effects. Second, chamber setup times can be modeled explicitly if chambers are allowed to change environments over time. Third, additional objectives such as energy consumption, labor availability, overtime, and prototype cost can be integrated into a multi-objective framework. Finally, a rolling-horizon version of TCSSOP would allow new projects to arrive dynamically while ongoing tests remain fixed.
