\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

TCSSOP combines three decisions that are usually studied apart: (i)~how to configure heterogeneous climate chambers that have multiple technical features, (ii)~how many physical samples to allocate to each development project, and (iii)~how to schedule the resulting multi-stage tests under eligibility, shared-environment, precedence, and due-date constraints. The closest literature is therefore industrial test-laboratory scheduling, R\&D prototype-test planning, and scheduling of reconfigurable or shared-environment resources. We review these streams in that order, stating after each what it explains in the refrigerator setting and what it still leaves open. Table~\ref{tab:literature_gap} summarizes the comparison.

The closest application stream is industrial test-laboratory scheduling. The Test Laboratory Scheduling Problem (TLSP) models laboratories in which multiple projects must be tested by qualified personnel on specialized equipment, subject to time windows, precedence, resource eligibility, and execution modes \citep{MischekMusliu2021,GeibingerMischekMusliu2024}. TLSP extends the resource-constrained project scheduling problem \citep{HartmannBriskorn2022,GomezSanchezLallaRuizFernandezGilCastroVoss2023} by a grouping stage in which smaller tasks are assembled into jobs before they are scheduled. Subsequent work develops constraint-programming models, very large neighborhood search, and local-search neighborhoods that modify both grouping and resource assignments \citep{MischekMusliuSchaerf2023,GeibingerMischekMusliu2024}. These studies matter because they address real test laboratories rather than generic shops: heterogeneous resources, eligibility, and project due dates appear together. Two structural differences remain. First, TLSP resources are personnel, workbenches, and equipment units; they are not climate chambers whose temperature, humidity, and voltage features must be chosen jointly and then shared by all stations inside the chamber. Second, TLSP grouping changes how existing tasks are bundled, whereas in refrigerator testing the sample count creates additional pull-down operations and simultaneously determines how many physical units are available for downstream parallelism. Thus TLSP captures laboratory scheduling with a given test set, but not decision-dependent workload generated by sample sizes, and not multi-feature chamber reconfiguration.

A second, equally close stream is prototype-based test planning in automotive R\&D, where the number of physical test units is itself a decision. \citet{BartelsZimmermann2009} schedule automotive tests so as to minimize the number of experimental vehicles, using a multi-mode resource-constrained project scheduling model with renewable and cumulative resources. \citet{ShiReichEpelmanKlampflCohn2017} assign tests to expensive prototype vehicles under compatibility restrictions and tight deadlines, showing that better packing of tests onto a limited fleet reduces time pressure without simply building more vehicles. Most recently, \citet{ClautiauxEssodaiguiNguyenSadykovYounes2025} jointly determine how many prototypes to build, which feature configuration each prototype should have, and which tests to assign to each unit, with the dual aim of reducing tardy tests and prototype count. These papers are the closest existing treatment of endogenous sample and prototype decisions in an R\&D testing context. The remaining gap is the resource that is being configured. In automotive studies, configuration refers to the prototype itself (for example, engine, body, or optional equipment), and the prototype is the machine on which tests are sequenced. In TCSSOP, the scarce configurable resources are climate chambers: a chamber can be set to one temperature--humidity environment at a time, only some chambers can provide humidity or voltage adjustment, and all stations inside a chamber inherit that shared environment. Sample size still matters, but it does so by changing chamber workload and station-level parallelism, not by defining the configuration of the test object.

The chamber side of the problem is closer to scheduling of reconfigurable machines and of shared-environment processors. In reconfigurable manufacturing, a machine or line can be placed in one of several configurations, and an operation is eligible only for compatible machine--configuration pairs. \citet{MahmoodjanlooTavakkoliMoghaddamBaboliBozorgiAmiri2020} study a flexible job shop with reconfigurable machine tools, where configuration-dependent setups arise when a machine switches state. \citet{DouSuZhao2020} jointly design the configuration of a reconfigurable flow line and schedule jobs on it, with a cost--tardiness trade-off. These models justify treating chamber environment as a planning decision rather than as a sequence-dependent setup that can be changed freely after every job. They do not, however, represent a chamber as a multi-station resource with several independent technical features---temperature, humidity, and voltage---that jointly determine eligibility, nor do they generate additional jobs through sample counts.

Shared environmental processing is instead prominent in burn-in and related laboratory batch settings. \citet{HulettDamodaranAmouie2017} schedule non-identical parallel batch processors to minimize total weighted tardiness, motivated by ovens in which jobs sharing a recipe occupy the same environmental resource. This is the closest analog of the chamber--station structure: several units are processed under one ambient condition, and incompatible conditions cannot be mixed. The analogy is nevertheless incomplete. Burn-in recipes are typically selected as a batching and sequencing decision for a given job set, whereas in refrigerator R\&D the chamber environment is held stable over the planning horizon because temperature and humidity transitions require long stabilization. Moreover, burn-in studies assume a fixed set of lots; they do not decide how many physical samples to build.

Once chamber environments are fixed, the remaining assignment-and-sequencing layer resembles unrelated parallel-machine scheduling with eligibility restrictions and tardiness. Machine eligibility, precedence, and additional resource constraints make these problems computationally hard and motivate specialized heuristics \citep{AfzaliradRezaeian2016,MaeckerShenMonch2023,UlagaDurasevicJakobovic2022}. Constructive due-date rules, including earliest due date (EDD), remain competitive when a decoder must be called repeatedly inside a search procedure \citep{PerezGonzalezFernandezViagasZamoraGarciaFraminan2019,WangHeKerkhoveVanhoucke2017}. Variable-neighborhood methods are effective for tardiness on unrelated parallel machines when neighborhoods are designed around assignment and sequencing \citep{DianaDeSouza2020,LiaoCheng2007}. These papers support the methodological design of TCSSOP---workload-based assignment, EDD decoding, and VNS diversification---but they all start from a fixed job set. Reliability demonstration testing is the complementary contrast: sample size is optimized, yet for statistical risk or cost, not for chamber-feasible execution \citep{Fernandez2022}.

Table~\ref{tab:literature_gap} makes the remaining gap precise. No existing study jointly (i)~configures chambers that have multiple reconfigurable features, (ii)~treats sample size as an endogenous generator of pull-down workload and of downstream parallelism, and (iii)~schedules the resulting tests on stations that share a chamber-level environment under stage-based refrigerator test logic. That combination is the problem addressed by TCSSOP.

To close this gap, this study formulates TCSSOP and develops an iterative framework that coordinates chamber-environment assignment, sample-size optimization, and feasible schedule construction. Unlike conventional VNS designs that perturb sequencing or machine assignment, the proposed diversification mechanism perturbs sample sizes and thereby explores alternative workload structures.
