\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

Industrial testing environments form a distinct class of scheduling problems. Multiple projects compete for specialized test resources, and feasibility depends not only on resource capacity but also on technical compatibility, test dependencies, and the availability of physical test units. Heterogeneous resources, due-date pressure, precedence relations, and eligibility restrictions make exact optimization difficult and motivate specialized heuristic approaches. These features are central to refrigerator R\&D testing, in which climatic chambers, physical samples, and multi-stage test portfolios must be coordinated. The closest literature can be grouped into four streams: scheduling in industrial test laboratories and thermal-chamber facilities; the allocation of expensive physical prototypes in R\&D testing; eligibility-restricted unrelated parallel-machine scheduling; and statistical reliability-test planning in which sample size is itself a decision.

A particularly relevant stream of research concerns scheduling in industrial test laboratories and product-testing facilities. \citet{MischekMusliu2021} study a real-world Test Laboratory Scheduling Problem (TLSP) in which testing activities require heterogeneous equipment and qualified personnel and are subject to temporal and resource-related constraints. An important characteristic of this setting is that individual tasks are grouped into jobs before detailed scheduling. Building on this structure, \citet{DanzingerEtAl2020} integrate variable task grouping with scheduling, while \citet{MischekMusliuSchaerf2023} further investigate local-search approaches for the combined problem. These studies demonstrate that test-laboratory scheduling can require decisions beyond conventional assignment and sequencing, particularly when heterogeneous resources and the organization of testing activities interact. Related applications emphasize additional features of physical testing. \citet{MartinEtAl2022}, for example, consider the scheduling of a predetermined series of product tests required for products intended to operate under extreme environmental conditions, incorporating technician-related constraints. Oven and thermal-chamber studies construct feasible batches or chamber runs under a shared processing environment: jobs may occupy the same resource only if their requirements are compatible \citep{LacknerMrkvickaMusliuWalkiewiczWinter2023,MajumderLahaSuganthan2018}. Such applications are closely related to climatic-chamber operations because test feasibility depends on specialized testing resources and test-specific operating conditions. Nevertheless, the workload to be executed is essentially determined before the scheduling decisions are made.

Another closely related research stream considers R\&D testing in which expensive physical prototypes must be allocated among multiple tests. \citet{BartelsZimmermann2009} study test scheduling in automotive R\&D projects, accounting for temporal dependencies and resource restrictions while seeking to reduce the number of experimental vehicles required. \citet{LimtanyakulSchwiegelshohn2012} similarly address the assignment and scheduling of dependent tests on vehicle prototypes using constraint-programming and hybrid approaches. \citet{ShiReichEpelmanKlampflCohn2017} examine prototype-vehicle test scheduling in an automotive product-development setting, where test--vehicle compatibility, timing restrictions, and the high cost of prototypes motivate the coordinated allocation of tests to physical vehicles. More recently, \citet{ClautiauxEssodaiguiNguyenSadykovYounes2025} jointly consider the number and configurations of prototype vehicles together with test assignment and sequencing. Their model lexicographically minimizes the number of late tests and then the number of prototypes used, thereby explicitly linking the planning of physical test resources with subsequent test execution. These studies establish that the number, characteristics, and allocation of physical test units can have a substantial effect on the resulting R\&D test schedule.

Climatic-chamber testing additionally requires the coordination of heterogeneous test resources whose technical capabilities and operating environments determine test eligibility. This feature relates to scheduling models with eligibility and resource restrictions. For example, \citet{AfzaliradRezaeian2016} consider unrelated parallel-machine scheduling with resource requirements, precedence constraints, sequence-dependent setups, and machine-eligibility restrictions, while \citet{MaeckerShenMonch2023} study an eligibility-constrained unrelated parallel-machine problem with a tardiness-oriented objective. These models provide relevant scheduling foundations for heterogeneous resources on which only subsets of operations are feasible. In climatic-chamber testing, however, eligibility also depends on a shared chamber-level environmental configuration: multiple stations within the same chamber operate under a common temperature--humidity setting, while chamber capabilities may further restrict humidity- or voltage-sensitive tests. Chamber configuration therefore determines the effective processing capacity available for different portions of the testing workload. Although configuration- and eligibility-dependent scheduling models capture parts of this interaction, they generally assume that the set of operations to be processed is known in advance.

A separate test-planning literature treats the number of physical test units as an endogenous decision. In reliability demonstration testing, \citet{Fernandez2022} jointly optimizes sample size, test duration, and acceptance criteria under statistical risk and cost considerations. Such models demonstrate that sample size need not be treated as a fixed input to test planning. Their focus, however, is primarily on the statistical design of the test rather than on the detailed execution of multiple projects on heterogeneous and capacity-constrained testing resources. Thus, reliability test-planning models endogenize sample quantity but abstract from detailed resource scheduling, whereas industrial laboratory and prototype-test scheduling models represent operational execution in greater detail but largely begin from a predetermined testing workload.

The problem considered in this study connects these research streams through a different role of the sample-size decision. For each refrigerator development project, an additional physical sample does not merely provide another unit on which an existing set of tests can be performed. Because the pull-down test must be performed for every physical sample, increasing the sample count also generates additional upstream testing workload. At the same time, the additional samples can be allocated to different downstream tests, increasing the opportunities for parallel execution. Conversely, reducing the sample count decreases pull-down workload but can restrict downstream parallelism. The effect of sample size on scheduling performance is therefore non-monotone and depends on project due dates, test requirements, physical-sample availability, stage-based precedence relations, and the distribution of capacity across compatible chamber environments.

This mechanism distinguishes the present setting from the individual research streams discussed above. Prototype-test scheduling models account for limited and costly physical test units and, in some cases, jointly determine their number or configuration, but the underlying portfolio of test operations remains predetermined. Industrial test-laboratory and thermal-test scheduling models capture heterogeneous resources, compatibility restrictions, and detailed execution constraints, but likewise treat the incoming testing workload as given or reorganize a predetermined set of testing tasks. Reliability-test planning explicitly optimizes sample size, but does not represent the resulting detailed chamber-level execution. In the present problem, by contrast, sample allocation simultaneously changes the amount of upstream work and the degree of downstream parallelism, while chamber-environment assignments determine where the resulting workload can be processed. The reviewed literature therefore does not capture this combination of decision-dependent workload generation, shared-environment chamber configuration, physical-sample availability, and detailed due-date-oriented test scheduling. This motivates an integrated formulation in which project-specific sample allocation, chamber configuration, and operational scheduling are coordinated rather than treated as independent planning decisions.
