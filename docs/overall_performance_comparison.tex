\subsection{Overall Performance Comparison}
\label{subsec:overall_results}

This section provides an integrated comparison of all benchmark variants and uses the results to justify the final configuration selected for the proposed framework. The comparison is organized around the design decisions that most directly affect solution quality: the intensity of VNS perturbation, the number of shake attempts allowed during diversification, and the contribution of each major algorithmic component. In addition to average performance, the discussion considers dispersion, best-instance counts, and runtime, because a configuration that performs well only on a subset of instances is less useful for tactical planning than one that delivers consistently strong results across heterogeneous scenarios.

The analysis proceeds in three steps. First, alternative VNS perturbation-ratio policies are compared under a common shake budget. This isolates the effect of perturbation intensity on the balance between diversification and solution preservation. Second, the selected ratio policy is evaluated under different shake budgets in order to determine whether additional diversification effort produces meaningful gains. Third, the resulting \textit{Full} configuration is compared against the main benchmark variants: the fixed-sample policy, the sample-optimization variant without outer-loop reconfiguration, and the variant without VNS diversification. This sequence makes it possible to distinguish parameter-tuning effects from the structural contribution of the proposed framework.

\paragraph{Comparison of VNS perturbation ratios.}
Table~\ref{tab:vns_ratio_comparison} reports the performance of alternative VNS ratio policies with a fixed shake budget of \(K_{\max}=100\). The average-gap values are reported as percentages, where lower values indicate better solution quality. The standard deviation captures how stable each policy is across the scenario set, while the best-instance count indicates how often a policy attains the best result among the compared alternatives.

The results show that using a single perturbation ratio is not sufficient to obtain consistently strong performance. Among the single-ratio variants, \(0.20\) performs best on average, with an average gap of \(18.11\%\). By contrast, \(0.10\) produces a larger average gap of \(29.62\%\), suggesting that very mild perturbations often fail to move the search out of the basin of attraction reached by the local improvement phase. The \(0.30\) ratio performs better than \(0.10\), but its average gap remains \(23.44\%\), which indicates that stronger perturbations alone can be too disruptive: they create diversity, but may also destroy useful sample-allocation and chamber-workload structures that have already been constructed.

The adaptive fallback policies improve robustness by applying perturbations sequentially instead of committing to a single disruption level. The \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy achieves the lowest average gap among the \(K_{\max}=100\) variants, with an average gap of \(14.93\%\), and also has the lowest standard deviation in this group. Relative to the best single-ratio policy, this corresponds to a further reduction in average gap while maintaining a controlled level of perturbation. This behavior is consistent with the intended role of the fallback mechanism: the search first attempts to improve the solution through a moderate perturbation, and only escalates to a stronger move when the moderate shake is insufficient.

The \(0.10\)--\(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy obtains the highest best-instance count, but this advantage does not translate into the strongest overall performance. Its average gap is \(22.06\%\), and its average runtime is \(66.0\) minutes, compared with \(14.93\%\) and \(49.7\) minutes for the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy. This result shows that best-instance count alone is not a sufficient selection criterion. A policy may occasionally find very strong solutions while still being less reliable on average. For this reason, the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) fallback structure was selected as the preferred VNS ratio policy: it provides the best combination of average quality, variability, and computational effort among the ratio alternatives.

\paragraph{Effect of the VNS shake budget.}
After selecting the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) fallback policy, the effect of the shake budget was evaluated by comparing \(K_{\max}=50\), \(100\), and \(200\). The shake budget controls how much opportunity the VNS phase has to generate and evaluate alternative sample structures after local improvement stagnates. The results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:vns_budget_comparison}.

Increasing the shake budget clearly improves both average performance and robustness. With \(K_{\max}=50\), the average gap is \(13.79\%\), indicating that the diversification phase is often terminated before sufficient alternative workload structures are explored. Increasing the budget to \(100\) reduces the average gap to \(5.65\%\) and increases the number of best-instance results from \(18\) to \(47\). The improvement is not limited to a few favorable cases: the standard deviation decreases from \(29.07\%\) to \(20.06\%\), showing that the additional shake attempts also reduce performance variability.

The best overall performance is obtained with \(K_{\max}=200\). This setting reduces the average gap to \(0.31\%\), decreases the standard deviation to \(1.65\%\), and achieves the best result in \(82\) out of \(90\) scenario combinations. The sharp reduction in standard deviation is particularly important because it indicates that the method is not merely improving the mean through a small number of large wins; rather, it becomes consistently close to the best observed solution across nearly the entire benchmark set.

The improvement comes at the cost of longer runtime. The average runtime increases from \(32.6\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=50\) to \(49.7\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=100\), and to \(85.1\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=200\). Nevertheless, this trade-off is acceptable for the planning context considered in this study. The framework is intended for tactical R\&D test planning, where solution quality and robustness are more important than real-time response. Moreover, the additional runtime produces a substantial reduction in both average gap and variability. Therefore, the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS policy with \(K_{\max}=200\) was selected as the final \textit{Full} configuration.

The results demonstrate that the final \textit{Full} configuration is substantially stronger than all main benchmark variants in terms of solution quality. The no-VNS variant has an average gap of \(178.67\%\) and does not obtain the best result in any scenario combination. This confirms the importance of diversification after local search and chamber reconfiguration have converged. Without VNS, the search can remain trapped in workload structures that cannot be improved through deterministic local moves alone. Although the no-VNS variant is much faster, its runtime advantage is obtained by omitting the mechanism that is responsible for escaping these stagnant regions of the search space.

The comparison with the no-outer variant highlights the importance of the feedback between sample-size decisions and chamber reconfiguration. The no-outer variant has an average gap of \(140.07\%\) relative to the final \textit{Full} configuration and is never the best-performing method. This result indicates that optimizing sample counts without repeatedly updating chamber settings is insufficient. Changes in the sample vector alter environment-specific workload distributions, voltage requirements, and chamber utilization patterns. If the chamber configuration is not updated in response, the algorithm evaluates sample decisions under a resource structure that may no longer be appropriate for the revised workload.

Finally, the fixed-sample policy performs substantially worse than the proposed framework. Its average gap is \(361.93\%\), and it does not produce the best solution in any of the \(90\) scenario combinations. Although the fixed policy is computationally very fast, its poor tardiness performance shows that the current industrial rule of assigning three samples to every product is not robust under heterogeneous due dates, test portfolios, and voltage requirements. The fixed rule ignores the fact that the marginal value of an additional sample differs across projects and test environments. As a result, it can allocate testing effort to products for which additional samples provide little scheduling benefit while failing to relieve bottlenecks for more critical projects.

Taken together, the benchmark results show that the proposed framework improves solution quality through three complementary mechanisms. First, sample-size optimization adjusts the workload structure according to project-level needs instead of imposing a uniform sampling rule. Second, outer-loop chamber reconfiguration updates the resource configuration in response to the revised workload distribution, allowing the system to adapt when sample decisions change the demand placed on chambers and environments. Third, VNS diversification explores alternative sample structures when local improvement stagnates, reducing the risk that the final solution is determined by an early local optimum.

The selected configuration therefore reflects a deliberate quality--runtime trade-off. The adaptive \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS policy avoids both insufficient and excessive perturbation, while \(K_{\max}=200\) provides enough diversification effort to make the results stable across the full benchmark set. Although this configuration requires more computational effort than the simplified variants, it delivers the most reliable solution quality and directly supports the main premise of the study: effective tactical test planning requires joint optimization of sample counts, chamber configuration, and diversification strategy rather than treating these decisions independently.
