\subsection{Overall Performance Comparison}
\label{subsec:overall_results}

This section compares all benchmark variants and explains the final configuration selected for the proposed framework. The analysis proceeds in three steps. First, alternative VNS perturbation-ratio policies are compared. Second, the effect of the VNS shake budget is evaluated. Third, the best VNS configuration is compared against the main benchmark variants, including the fixed-sample policy, the sample-optimization variant without outer-loop reconfiguration, and the variant without VNS diversification.

\paragraph{Comparison of VNS perturbation ratios.}
Table~\ref{tab:vns_ratio_comparison} reports the performance of alternative VNS ratio policies with a fixed shake budget of \(K_{\max}=100\). The average-gap values are reported as percentages, where lower values indicate better solution quality.

The results show that using a single perturbation ratio is not sufficient to obtain consistently strong performance. Among the single-ratio variants, \(0.20\) performs best on average, with an average gap of \(18.11\%\), whereas \(0.10\) and \(0.30\) lead to larger gaps of \(29.62\%\) and \(23.44\%\), respectively. This indicates that very mild perturbations do not provide enough diversification, while stronger perturbations may disrupt useful workload structures generated by the local search.

The adaptive fallback policies improve robustness by applying perturbations sequentially. In particular, the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy achieves the lowest average gap among the \(K_{\max}=100\) variants, with an average gap of \(14.93\%\) and the lowest standard deviation among the ratio policies. This suggests that starting from a moderate perturbation level and moving to a stronger level only when necessary provides a better balance between diversification and solution stability. Although the \(0.10\)--\(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy obtains the highest number of best-instance results, its average gap and runtime are worse than those of the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) policy. Therefore, the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) fallback structure was selected as the preferred VNS ratio policy.

\paragraph{Effect of the VNS shake budget.}
After selecting the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) fallback policy, the effect of the shake budget was evaluated by comparing \(K_{\max}=50\), \(100\), and \(200\). The results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:vns_budget_comparison}.

Increasing the shake budget clearly improves both average performance and robustness. With \(K_{\max}=50\), the average gap is \(13.79\%\), indicating that the diversification phase is often terminated before sufficient alternative workload structures are explored. Increasing the budget to \(100\) reduces the average gap to \(5.65\%\) and increases the number of best-instance results from \(18\) to \(47\). The best overall performance is obtained with \(K_{\max}=200\), which reduces the average gap to \(0.31\%\), decreases the standard deviation to \(1.65\%\), and achieves the best result in \(82\) out of \(90\) scenario combinations.

The improvement comes at the cost of longer runtime. The average runtime increases from \(32.6\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=50\) to \(49.7\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=100\), and to \(85.1\) minutes for \(K_{\max}=200\). Nevertheless, the additional computational effort is justified in this study because the main objective is high-quality tactical planning rather than real-time dispatching. Moreover, the large increase in best-instance count and the sharp reduction in variability indicate that \(K_{\max}=200\) provides a more reliable final configuration. Therefore, the \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS policy with \(K_{\max}=200\) was selected as the final \textit{Full} configuration.

The results demonstrate that the final \textit{Full} configuration dominates all main benchmark variants in terms of solution quality. Compared with the no-VNS variant, the average gap is \(178.67\%\), and the no-VNS method does not obtain the best result in any scenario combination. This confirms the importance of diversification after local search and chamber reconfiguration have converged. Without VNS, the search may remain trapped in workload structures that cannot be improved through deterministic local moves alone.

The comparison with the no-outer variant also highlights the importance of the feedback between sample-size decisions and chamber reconfiguration. The no-outer variant has an average gap of \(140.07\%\) relative to the final \textit{Full} configuration and is never the best-performing method. This result indicates that optimizing sample counts without repeatedly updating chamber settings is insufficient, because changes in the sample vector alter environment-specific workload distributions and may require a new chamber configuration.

Finally, the fixed-sample policy performs substantially worse than the proposed framework. Its average gap is \(361.93\%\), and it does not produce the best solution in any of the \(90\) scenario combinations. Although the fixed policy is computationally very fast, its poor tardiness performance shows that the current industrial rule of assigning three samples to every product is not robust under heterogeneous due dates, test portfolios, and voltage requirements. The result strongly supports the main premise of this study: sample counts should be treated as decision variables rather than fixed operational inputs.

Overall, the benchmark results show that the proposed framework improves solution quality through three complementary mechanisms. First, sample-size optimization adjusts the workload structure according to project-level needs. Second, outer-loop chamber reconfiguration updates the resource configuration in response to the revised workload distribution. Third, VNS diversification explores alternative sample structures when local improvement stagnates. The best-performing configuration is the adaptive \(0.20\)--\(0.30\) VNS policy with \(K_{\max}=200\), which provides the most robust performance across all scenario combinations, at the cost of higher but still acceptable computational effort for tactical R\&D test planning.
